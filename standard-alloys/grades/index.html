04

The six families

Austenitic manganese steel

Hadfield steel. Standard 12–14% Mn, with 18% and 22% Mn types for heavier work hardening.

ASTM A128IS 276EN 10349
Chemistry
About 1.0–1.4% carbon with 11–14% manganese; higher-manganese types and chromium or molybdenum additions for harder, more abrasive rock.
Hardness
About 190–240 HB as water-quenched. The working face hardens to over 500 HB under repeated impact while the core stays tough.
Heat treatment
Solution-treated above 1,000 °C and quenched in water. Our castings leave the furnace and enter the quench in about 45 seconds.
Strength
Very high toughness. The part gets harder where it is struck, so it resists gouging without becoming brittle.
Limits
Needs impact to harden: under sliding abrasion with little impact it wears quickly. It is non-magnetic, so surfaces are checked with dye penetrant, not magnetic particles.

Cr-Mo and martensitic steels

Pearlitic and martensitic low-alloy wear steels.

IS 4896Customer specification
Chemistry
Medium- and high-carbon steels alloyed with chromium and molybdenum, with nickel added for hardenability in heavy sections.
Hardness
Typically 300–400 HB for pearlitic mill-liner grades and 450–550 HB or more for martensitic grades, set by heat treatment.
Heat treatment
Normalised or hardened in air or oil, then tempered to the required balance of hardness and toughness.
Strength
The middle ground: harder than manganese steel without needing impact, yet much tougher than white iron.
Limits
Outworn by white iron under pure abrasion and less forgiving than manganese steel when tramp metal enters.

High-chromium white iron

Chromium–molybdenum white irons, typically 15–28% Cr.

ASTM A532 Class II and IIIIS 4771ISO 21988 / EN 12513
Chemistry
About 2–3.6% carbon with 11–30% chromium, plus molybdenum, nickel or copper so that heavy sections harden through.
Hardness
Typically 550–700 HB (about 58–64 HRC) after hardening.
Heat treatment
Destabilised at high temperature, air-hardened and tempered.
Strength
Hard chromium carbides in a martensitic matrix give the longest life against sliding and low-stress abrasion.
Limits
Low impact toughness: not for tramp metal or heavy blows. Thick sections need the alloy and heat treatment designed together.

Ni-Hard (nickel-chromium white iron)

Ni-Hard 1, 2 and 4 types.

ASTM A532 Class IIS 4771ISO 21988 / EN 12513
Chemistry
About 2.5–3.6% carbon with 3–7% nickel and 1.4–11% chromium; Ni-Hard 4 is the higher-chromium type.
Hardness
Typically 500–650 HB.
Heat treatment
Stress-relieved or hardened, depending on type and section.
Strength
Hard and abrasion-resistant, with a long service record in coal grinding.
Limits
Low impact toughness, like all white irons. Usually outlasted by high-chromium iron in pure abrasion.

Heat-resistant steels

Cr-Ni types HH (25Cr-12Ni), HK (25Cr-20Ni) and HP40Nb (25Cr-35Ni, niobium-modified), high-chromium heat-resistant steels and stainless grades. Nickel and chromium can be tailored to the duty, subject to a feasibility review.

IS 4522IS 519ASTM A297ASTM A447EN 10295
Chemistry
HH: about 25% chromium and 12% nickel. HK: about 25% chromium and 20% nickel. HP40Nb: about 25% chromium and 35% nickel with niobium, for the highest temperatures and carburising gas. Where the duty needs it, we tailor nickel and chromium beyond the standard ranges after a feasibility review. Chromium forms the protective oxide; nickel keeps the structure austenitic and strong at temperature.
Temperature
Holds its shape and resists scaling at temperatures that would burn ordinary steel away. Typical limits in oxidising service are about 1,100 °C for HH and 1,150 °C for HK.
Heat treatment
Usually supplied as cast. Chemistry is held to tight limits because it sets life at temperature.
Limits
Thermal cycling, load and sulphur lower the practical limit. Long service at 650–900 °C can embrittle some HH types. That is why the choice between HH and HK is made by position.

Carbon and low-alloy steels

Structural cast steels, normalised or quenched and tempered.

ASTM A27ASTM A148IS 1030IS 2644EN 10293
Chemistry
Plain carbon and low-alloy steels with manganese, chromium, nickel and molybdenum as the strength class requires.
Hardness
Typically 150–300 HB as normalised; higher when quenched and tempered.
Heat treatment
Normalised, or quenched and tempered to the specified strength.
Strength
Strength, toughness and weldability for parts that carry load, locate other parts or are machined to fit.
Limits
Not a wear grade on its own: where it meets abrasion, the design protects or hardens the surface.

Ask a metallurgist

Seven mistakes when buying wear castings

And what to ask your foundry instead.

  • Grade picked by habit, not by dutyMatch the alloy to the feed, the impact and the temperature the part will meet.
  • Manganese steel quenched too slowlyA slow transfer from furnace to water leaves brittle carbides.
  • No method before the patternGating and feeding should be designed and simulated for critical parts, before metal is poured.
  • Wrong alloy in the hot zoneHH and HK look alike on a drawing. Ask for the spectrometer report against the heat.
  • Raw tolerances acceptedProof-machine and check the fit, so the part installs first time in a shutdown.
  • No record of the heatInsist on chemistry, hardness and NDT reports traceable to the heat number.
  • Waiting on overseas lead timesWork with a foundry that already holds your patterns.
